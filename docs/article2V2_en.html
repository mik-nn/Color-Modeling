Where Five Patches Fail — and Why

Michal Erlich
Color Management Researcher & Profiling Tool Developer

This is a direct sequel to Predictive Color Adaptation on New Media. That article showed you can adapt a color profile to a new substrate from about five well-chosen patches. This one is about the other ~12 % — the substrate pairs where five patches, or fifty, are not enough: what breaks there, and why it is usually the symptom of a fixable print-setup problem, not a limit of the method.

What the first article showed — and what it left open

Few-patch substrate adaptation works well. With a fully characterized reference profile and a handful of measured patches on a new substrate, a Paper-Ratio predictor reconstructs the rest of the target's spectra. The headline results, on 27 art papers across two printers (Epson SureColor SC-P9000 and Canon G2470):

  • About five strategically placed patches reach a median ΔE₀₀ ≤ 1.5 with the 95th percentile ≤ 3.0 on roughly 88–90 % of substrate pairs within a single print mode.
  • Which patches you measure matters more than how many — an optimizer-selected 5-patch layout beats a hand-picked 12-patch one.

But one limitation remained: roughly 12 % of pairs fail the quality criterion regardless of patch budget — more measurements do not rescue them. This article explains why. The failures turn out to be neither random nor mysterious: they have a consistent location, a measurable signature, and two distinct physical causes.

Experimental constraint (unchanged from article 1). All experiments run on Epson SC-P9000 and Canon G2470/G1430 under standard RGB drivers: color separation happens inside the driver's LUTs and is not directly controllable. Optical brightener (OBA) fluorescence is handled as a preprocessing factor. Everything is reproducible from the open tool at github.com/mik-nn/Color-Modeling.

1. The failure is chromatic: chroma and hue, not lightness

The first question about any failed prediction is what kind of error it is. A profile that prints too dark is a very different problem from one that has shifted hue.

Decompose the largest errors (patches with ΔE₀₀ > 3) into the standard CIELAB components: lightness |ΔL*|, chroma |ΔC*| and hue |ΔH*|. The answer is blunt. On Canon, 148 of 626 same-family pairs fail the criterion — and in 147 of those 148 pairs (99 %) the chromatic error dominates: on the bad patches, mean |ΔC*| ≈ 3.3 and |ΔH*| ≈ 3.1 outweigh |ΔL*| ≈ 1.0 by nearly 5×. Epson SC-P9000 shows the same pattern. Whatever is going wrong, it is a chroma-and-hue shift at the edge of the gamut, not a tone-reproduction error.

This already rules out a whole class of fixes. Lightness errors are what more patches and better interpolation repair well: the predictor is smooth, and smooth models are good at lightness. A chromatic shift that the multiplicative model cannot express is structural — and indeed, a best-case attack (a genetic algorithm optimizing the patch set directly on the failing pairs) recovers about half of them and then plateaus, with every remaining failure still chroma-dominated. Patch placement is not the bottleneck here.

2. The error concentrates where ink coverage is highest

If the error is chromatic and lives at the gamut edge — where exactly is that edge? It is where the most ink goes down.

A word on where C, M, Y come from in an RGB dataset. The printers in this study are driven through RGB: the file carries R, G, B values, and the separation into actual inks happens inside the driver's closed LUTs, so the true ink laydown is not observable. The direction of the relationship is known, though: the lower a channel value, the more ink the printer deposits. So C = 255−R, M = 255−G, Y = 255−B serve as effective ink-amount coordinates, and their sum C+M+Y is the RGB-driver analogue of the familiar total ink coverage (TAC — Total Area Coverage). It is an estimate in driver units, not physical ink consumption — but for our purposes that is enough.

Rank every test patch by its total ink coverage and correlate with the prediction error. On the canonical hard pair DecorMatte → ChromataWhite (both Canvas Matte substrates), the Spearman rank correlation between coverage and error is 0.71. Bucketed, the trend is stark:

The pattern is not specific to one pair: across the whole dataset the mean error climbs with coverage the same way — from ≈1.1 ΔE₀₀ in the highlights to ≈3.3 on patches with all three inks at full. In the light and mid ranges the method sits comfortably inside spec; the error doubles in the high range and quadruples at maximum ink. The few-patch method is accurate everywhere except where ink is laid down most heavily. That is the hint: the problem is not the model's reach, but what the ink physically does when there is too much of it.

3. Mechanism A — over-inking: chroma rolls off past its peak

Watch what happens to chroma as you add ink along a single-color gradation ramp. On a properly configured substrate, chroma climbs and then levels off: past some point, additional ink stops adding saturation. On many substrates something worse happens: chroma climbs, peaks, and then falls, while the hue drifts. This is the textbook signature of exceeding the ink limit: the surplus colorant no longer lays down cleanly — it spreads and overprints unpredictably, muddying the color.

Concretely — the green ramp (cyan + yellow inks) on Signa 270. Here t is the relative ink amount along the ramp; the levels are the actual chart patches nearest to the nominal 0–1 fractions:

Ink amount t0.000.180.380.630.751.00
Chroma C*ab0.017.440.275.782.870.4

Chroma peaks at t* = 0.75 and loses 12.5 units on the way to full ink. The last quarter of the ink does not deepen the color — it desaturates it and drifts the hue. The chroma-peak coverage t* is, in effect, a perceptual ink limit for that ink direction. Of the 24 P9000 profiles measured under M0, 23 show this roll-off on at least one ramp.

The clearest way to see it is to plot chroma against ink amount (Fig. 3). The Signa 270 curve tops out at t* = 0.75 and turns back down; the hue drifts too — moderately on this ramp (≈4°), while on the worst patches in the dataset the hue shift reaches 13–18°. On a substrate without over-inking the curve climbs monotonically all the way to full ink.

Every hue has its own chroma maximum

Here is a detail that is easy to miss. Each hue has its own chroma maximum — and reaches it at its own ink amount. The green ramp (hue ≈ 130°) peaks at one coverage, the red one (≈ 30°) at another, the blue one (≈ 270°) at a third. A single ink limit "for all colors" does not exist.

So two related but distinct quantities do the work:

  • The ink amount at the peak, t* = arg maxt C*ab(t). It is specific to each ink direction (the pure C, M, Y ramps, the two-ink overprints R, G, B, and the neutral ramp) and defines the ink limit in that direction. A patch is over the limit if it carries more ink than the chroma maximum of its own direction.
  • The maximum chroma C*ab at that point. It is computed separately for each of 36 hue sectors (10° steps). Together the 36 values form the gamut boundary in the a*b* plane.

In other words, both the ink limit and the gamut boundary are hue-resolved: data is not cut at a single global threshold, but at each direction's own chroma maximum.

What an ink limit costs in gamut

If you cap the ink at t* — keep only the patches at or below each direction's chroma peak — how much gamut do you lose? Compare the gamut volume (the convex hull in Lab) before and after. The median loss is 5.4 % (over the 65 profiles with the most pronounced roll-off, across nine ink systems; on the clean P9000 profiles it is 1.6–4.5 %). Meanwhile the gamut boundary — the maximum chroma in each of the 36 hue sectors — is preserved essentially in full (median retention 100.0 %).

What you lose is shadow volume only — and without distorting the gamut's shape. Patches past the chroma peak do not extend the gamut outward; they densify its dark interior. After the limit, the lower half of the gamut body is, in effect, scaled slightly toward the center: the black point rises by a few L* units, the side walls barely move, and the widest points of the body — the chroma maxima — do not move at all. Giving up the over-limit patches costs not a single unit of chroma; what is sacrificed is a thin layer of the deepest shadow density, which was unstable to begin with.

Sidebar — two meanings of "apply an ink limit." Rescale: make t* the new 100 %, so the driver re-maps the full device range into [0, t*] — this is the corrected, ink-limited print mode. Mask: leave the print mode alone and simply stop trusting (or printing) colors past t*. The numbers in this article are the masked regime — every statistic is computed on the in-limit subset of the original prints; nothing was re-printed. The achievable gamut is the same either way (you deposit at most t* ink), so the 5.4 % figure holds for both. The distinction matters only for prediction validity: a profile characterized in the original over-inked mode does not automatically describe the rescaled one — characterizing the corrected mode needs new prints. The point is that the rescaled mode is not some exotic unknown; it is the right mode, and the original over-inked mode is the misconfiguration.

4. Mechanism B — ink holdout: a media-preset mismatch

There is one substrate in the set that defeats every model I have built, and it is the most instructive of all: DecorMatte, a matte canvas (Canvas Matte). It is the source of the hardest cases in the first article — and the single one of the 24 profiles with no chroma roll-off. Its green ramp (same actual ink levels as above):

Ink amount t0.000.180.380.630.751.00
Chroma C*ab0.018.135.960.470.972.1

Chroma rises monotonically all the way to full ink — the maximum sits at maximum coverage. By the chroma-peak criterion, DecorMatte is not over-inked. Yet it is the hardest substrate to predict. So the cause here is different.

It is ink holdout: on this matte canvas, ink at high coverage does not absorb — it sits on the surface. In parts of the spectrum the reflectance rises where, on well-absorbing substrates, it falls. Chroma keeps climbing because the surface ink layer is still chromatic, but the spectral shape of such a patch diverges from its shape on any absorbing substrate — and a transform learned from absorbing substrates predicts it poorly. This is not an ink overload to be cured with an ink limit; it is a mismatch between the medium and the selected mode, cured by choosing the correct media preset in the driver.

Two pieces of evidence that mechanisms A and B are independent. First: DecorMatte's monotone ramp versus the roll-off on everyone else. Second: the descriptor of roll-off magnitude and the neutral-ramp curvature descriptor (the holdout signature) are essentially uncorrelated — 0.03. They measure different phenomena.

5. Prediction error as a print-mode quality indicator

Now the result that ties everything together — the one I validated most carefully.

The ink limit was derived blind — from the profile's own chroma curve alone, with no knowledge of the cross-substrate transfer error. So ask: where is that error actually largest? It turns out 70 % of the worst 5 % of patches by ΔE₀₀ lie above that limit — where more ink is laid down than at the chroma maximum. A limit computed with no reference to the error lands squarely on the region where the transfer breaks down.

That suggests an operational move: report the in-limit patches and flag the rest as unreliable. Doing so lifts the pass rate from 71.3 % to 79.6 %. But that gain needs a control: shrink any test set and its pass rate rises mechanically — fewer patches, fewer chances to fail. So I dropped the same number of random patches instead, averaged over 30 seeds:

Test setPass rate
Full (no exclusions)71.3 %
Random patches dropped (matched count)71.6 %
Over-limit patches dropped79.6 %

Random exclusion buys +0.3 pp — essentially nothing. Excluding specifically the over-limit patches buys +8 pp over the random baseline. The gain is not test-set shrink: the over-limit region genuinely is where the transfer fails. More important still, the limit is computed on the reference profile — the one that is fully characterized at deployment — so the technique applies up front, not in hindsight.

Read correctly, this is not "the method covers a smaller gamut." It is a per-patch quality indicator: a large prediction error says the profile was built in an over-inked (or otherwise unsuitable) print mode — and roughly shows where.

6. The fixes are standard print preparation

The encouraging part is that both mechanisms are cured by long-established procedures — nothing new needs inventing.

Over-inking → ink limiting. The chroma-peak point t* is a perceptual per-channel ink limit, and setting proper per-channel and total ink limits is a solved, standard step of print-mode setup (see the earlier piece Pre-calibration of RGB Printers). Set the limits, re-linearize — and the problem region simply stops being printed; the profile you then build is cleaner and lives in a predictable regime. One honesty note: all the numbers in this article come from the original prints — over-limit patches were excluded from evaluation, not re-printed with a capped laydown; characterizing a profile rebuilt in the ink-limited mode is separate work requiring new prints. What this article adds is a way to detect that a limit is needed straight from the spectra of an existing profile, and to show that it costs almost nothing in gamut (≈5 % of shadow volume, with the chroma boundary essentially intact).

Ink holdout → the correct media preset. DecorMatte does not need an ink cap; it needs a mode matched to a non-absorbing matte canvas. The detector flags it just as clearly — as the one substrate with a monotone ramp — so you know at once not to reach for the ink-limit tool.

What remains genuinely open is the residual chroma-and-hue ceiling that survives after the print mode is corrected: a per-ink interaction that the multiplicative paper-ratio model structurally cannot express. That will need either a per-ink chromatic model or a CMYK-addressable dataset, where the separation is visible instead of hidden by the driver. That is the subject of the next article.

Takeaways

  1. When few-patch substrate adaptation fails, the error lies in chroma and hue, at the gamut edge, at maximum ink coverage — not in lightness, and not in the patch count.
  2. That edge hides two distinct mechanisms, both symptoms of printing in an unsuitable mode: exceeding the ink limit (chroma falls past its peak) and ink holdout (ink sits on the surface; DecorMatte — the only substrate with no roll-off).
  3. The chroma maximum t* is hue-specific; there is no single limit for all colors. The 36 per-hue-sector chroma maxima together form the gamut boundary. Capping ink at t* costs ~5 % of shadow volume while keeping the chroma boundary essentially intact.
  4. Cross-substrate prediction error works as a print-mode quality indicator: a limit computed with no knowledge of the error captures 70 % of the worst patches, and excluding them is worth +8 pp over a matched random-exclusion control — a real, deployable signal, not a gamut concession.
  5. The remedies — ink limiting and the correct media preset — are standard prepress practice, not a new method. The open problem is the chroma-and-hue ceiling that persists even in a correct print mode.

All numbers are reproducible from the open tool at github.com/mik-nn/Color-Modeling; the key scripts are h45_inklimit_gamut.ts and h45c_control.ts; the hue-resolved chroma boundary is computed in gamutVolume.ts (maxChromaPerHueBin), the per-direction ink limit in inkLimitChroma.ts (chromaMaxT).
